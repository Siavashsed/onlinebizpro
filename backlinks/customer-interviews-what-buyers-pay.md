**Title:** 17 of 22 interviewees said they'd pay $49/month. Two actually did. What I changed in how I ask about price

A few years back I ran 22 customer interviews for a scheduling add-on aimed at physio clinics. Most were early morning Zoom calls before their first patients arrived. Seventeen of them told me they'd happily pay $49 a month. When it came time to actually charge, two paid.

I don't think anyone lied to me. I asked hypothetical questions, so I got hypothetical answers. Saying yes to an imaginary price costs nothing, and most people want to be nice to the person who just spent 30 minutes asking about their problems.

Now I don't ask about price at all. I ask about money they've already spent:

- What do you use for this right now, and what does it cost?
- When did you last pay to fix this problem?
- Who approved that purchase, and how long did it take?

If someone has never spent a dollar or an hour on the problem, their "I'd pay for that" means very little. Past spending is evidence. Future intent is a guess.

Has anyone found other questions that reliably separate real buyers from polite ones?

## QUORA
**Answer this question:** How do I find out what customers will actually pay for my product before I build it?

Stop asking them what they would pay. That question feels like the obvious one, but it gives you the least reliable data you can collect.

I learned this the expensive way. In March 2019 I ran 22 interviews for a scheduling add-on for physio clinics, mostly on 7am Zoom calls before their first patients came in. Seventeen of them told me they'd happily pay $49 a month. When I launched, two paid.

Nobody was lying. The problem is that a hypothetical question gets a hypothetical answer, and hypothetical money costs nothing. People are also polite. After 30 minutes of you listening to their problems, saying "yes, I'd pay for that" is the friendly thing to do.

What works better is asking about money they have already spent:

1. **What do you use for this today?** A spreadsheet, a competitor, a part-time assistant. Whatever it is, that's your real competition and your price anchor.
2. **What does that cost you?** In money, hours, or both. If the answer is "nothing, really," the problem may not hurt enough.
3. **When did you last pay to fix this?** A recent purchase means a live budget. No purchase ever means you'd be creating a new budget line, which is much harder.
4. **Who approved it, and how long did it take?** This tells you about the sales cycle you are walking into.

Then test commitment, not opinion. Ask for a pre-order, a deposit, or a signed letter of intent. Someone who will put down even $20 tells you more than ten people who say "sounds great."
