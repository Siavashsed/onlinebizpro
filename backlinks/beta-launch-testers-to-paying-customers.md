**Title:** Anyone else notice your beta testers go quiet the moment you mention pricing?

I ran a 214-person beta for a scheduling tool back in 2019. It was free for ten weeks and the feedback was great: feature requests, kind notes, people telling me they loved it. I took that as proof we had something.

Then I switched on a $29 monthly plan on a Tuesday morning and watched who actually stayed. It was a small slice of the group, and it wasn't the people who had sent me the longest feedback emails. The loudest fans mostly vanished. The ones who paid were often quiet users who simply used the tool every week to get something done.

The lesson I took from it: free feedback and willingness to pay are two different signals, and a free beta only measures the first. If you wait until the end to talk about price, you've spent weeks collecting compliments instead of evidence.

Now I put a price in front of testers early, even if it's just a 'this will be $X after beta' line and a card on file option, and I watch who leans in. It makes the beta feel more like a sales process with a product attached.

Curious how others here handle it. Do you charge from day one, or keep it free until launch?

## QUORA
**Answer this question:** How do I convert beta testers into paying customers?

The short version: stop treating your beta as a free trial period and start treating it as a sales process with a product attached.

I'm Ryan Cross, and I've run several betas as a founder and advisor. The most instructive one was in March 2019: a 214-person beta for a scheduling tool, free for ten weeks. The feedback was lovely. Then I switched on a $29 monthly plan on a Tuesday morning, and the results were humbling. Only a small share converted, and they weren't the people who'd sent the warmest messages.

Here's what I'd do differently, and what I now tell founders I advise:

1. Put a price on the table early. Don't wait until the last week. Tell testers from the start what it will cost after beta. Their reaction is data.

2. Watch behavior, not compliments. The people who use the product weekly without being nudged are your likely buyers. Enthusiastic emailers often aren't.

3. Ask directly. Testers rarely volunteer to pay. Send a clear, personal message with a link to subscribe, and make it easy to say yes or no.

4. Offer a real reason to commit now, such as a founding-member rate locked in for life. Keep it honest and limited.

5. Talk to the ones who say no. A short call asking what would make it worth paying for is often the most valuable hour of the whole beta.

6. Keep the beta short. Ten weeks was too long. Long free periods build habits of not paying.

The core idea is that feedback and willingness to pay are different signals, and only one of them keeps a business alive.
