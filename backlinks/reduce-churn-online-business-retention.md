**Title:** Is most of your churn actually failed payments, not people choosing to cancel?

I ran a $29/month template subscription back in 2019 with about 410 paying members, and we were losing roughly 11% of them every month. My instinct was that people didn't feel enough loyalty, so I spent six weeks building a loyalty tier. It barely moved the number.

When I finally dug into the cancellation data, a big chunk of the people leaving hadn't chosen to leave at all. Their cards had failed (expired, declined, a bank flagging the charge) and we had no real recovery process. We sent one generic email and then quietly canceled them.

Two other things stood out. A lot of the churn happened in the first week or two, when new members never got to a first win with the product. And the people who did actively cancel were rarely angry. They'd just drifted.

Fixing the boring stuff did far more than the loyalty tier: card retry timing, better dunning emails, a card-update link that worked in one click, and a tighter onboarding sequence.

Curious whether others have split their churn into voluntary vs. involuntary. What did you find, and what moved the needle for you?

## QUORA
**Answer this question:** How can I reduce churn in my online subscription business?

Start by splitting your churn into two buckets: voluntary (customers who decided to cancel) and involuntary (customers whose payments failed). Most founders only look at the first bucket, and that's usually a mistake.

I learned this the hard way. In 2019 I was running a $29-a-month template subscription with around 410 members, and we were losing about 11% every month. I assumed people weren't engaged enough, so I spent six weeks building a loyalty tier. It barely moved the number. When I finally audited the data, a big portion of the losses were failed cards that we never properly tried to recover.

Here's the order I'd work in now:

1. Fix failed payments first. Set up smart retries (spaced over several days rather than all at once), send a short sequence of dunning emails, and give people a one-click link to update their card. This is often the cheapest win available.

2. Fix the first week. Many cancellations happen before a customer has had any real result. Map the one action that predicts someone sticking around, then build your onboarding to get people to it fast.

3. Talk to the people who actually cancel. A single exit question (what was the main reason?) tells you more than a dashboard. You'll often find it's price timing, a missing feature, or simply that they forgot why they signed up.

4. Only then consider loyalty perks, annual plans, or discounts. Those work best once you've stopped the accidental leakage.
