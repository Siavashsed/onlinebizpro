Google's March 2024 core update cut low-quality results by 45% and gutted thousands of sites. What it meant for online businesses.

Google's March 2024 core update ended the era of cheap search traffic. If your online business lived on SEO, it probably hit you before you understood what it was. I watched it happen live. On March 8, a little after 6 a.m., I opened Search Console for a kitchen gear affiliate site I'd been advising and saw clicks down 38% from the Friday before. By April 2 the drop was 71%, and the founder had stopped answering Slack before noon. Here's my position now that it's August 2024 and things have settled: the update was brutal and sloppy in places, and it was still mostly right. The businesses that got hurt worst weren't unlucky. They'd built a company on a channel they didn't own, using content nobody needed.

The scale is what made this one different. Google rolled the update out over 45 days, finished on April 19, and claimed it removed close to half the junk from its results. That's an unusually large, specific number for Google to put out in public. You only make a claim like that when you mean it as a warning, and plenty of founders I know learned that the hard way.

What Google Actually Changed in March 2024

On March 5, Google published two announcements at once: one on the Google Search Central blog and one from search VP Elizabeth Tucker on The Keyword. The promise was specific. Google expected the update, combined with earlier work, to cut low-quality, unoriginal content in results by 40%. After the rollout finished, the company updated its post to say the real figure was 45%.

The bigger change was structural. The helpful content system had run as a separate classifier since August 2022, and in March it got folded into the core ranking systems. Sounds like plumbing. It wasn't. Before, a helpful content hit was a sitewide penalty you could at least name. After March there was no separate signal to point at... your site just ranked worse everywhere, and nobody would tell you which pages were dragging it down.

The three spam policies worth taping to your monitor

Google also published new rules in its spam policies documentation:

  • Scaled content abuse: publishing lots of pages mainly to rank, whether a human, an AI or a mix of both wrote them.
  • Site reputation abuse: third-party pages on a strong domain that borrow its authority, like the coupon sections on news sites. Enforcement began May 5.
  • Expired domain abuse: buying an old domain for its backlinks and filling it with unrelated content.

Read the first one twice. Google didn't ban AI content. It banned the business model of publishing at volume for a search engine instead of a reader. The tool was never the target. The intent was.

Who Got Hit, and Why Founders Were Furious

The founder groups I'm in went through the stages fast. First came disbelief, then Search Console graphs falling off a cliff, posted with no caption because none was needed. In mid-March I checked about a dozen sites by hand that had disappeared from the index completely, with a site: search returning nothing at all. Those were manual actions, not algorithm shifts. Most belonged to people who knew exactly what they'd been doing.

But the anger didn't all come from spammers, and you deserve the full picture. HouseFresh, a small air purifier review site that actually buys and tests products, had already published a February 2024 piece showing how Google rewarded big media brands' thin "best of" lists over independent testers. After March, honest sites like that took damage too. Reddit threads also started showing up in the top spots for queries where a tested review would serve you far better. For more on this, see Apple's ATT Update Broke the Facebook Ad Machine in 2021.

So two things are true at once. Google cleared out a lot of junk, and it handed a chunk of that traffic to giant domains and user-generated content. Some useful small publishers paid for that. Then in July, the SparkToro and Datos 2024 zero-click study put numbers on the squeeze: for every 1,000 US Google searches, only 374 clicks go to the open web, and 58.5% of searches end with no click at all. Your share of organic search was shrinking before any update touched you.

The AI Content Bet I Watched Blow Up

When ChatGPT went mainstream, a lot of online sellers used it like a content printer. I wrote about what ChatGPT actually meant for online sellers in 2023, and this update is the honest footnote to that piece. The cheap-content math held up for about 14 months. Then it collapsed.

I'll own my part. In mid-2023 I told a client in the home office niche to publish 400 AI-assisted buying guides over six months, at around $11 of editing time per article. Traffic went from roughly 9,000 monthly sessions to 61,000 by January 2024, and affiliate revenue reached about $4,200 a month. After March it fell to around $600. We'd built nothing a reader would bookmark. Every page was a decent summary of other pages, and Google finally had a policy name for that.

My buddy Marcus runs a recipe site, and honestly he took this harder than anyone I know. His April fix was a $29 "AI humanizer" tool he ran all 900 posts through. If you've never seen what those spit out, it turns "add the onions" into something like "introduce the alliums to the vessel." He texted me one at 1 a.m. with just "is this better??" and I laughed loud enough to wake my dog. I'm not sure what the lesson is. Maybe panic makes you do dumb things fast. Maybe I've done the same with other tools and just never got caught. Probably both.

Here's the misconception I keep hearing: "Google detects AI and punishes it." From what I've seen, not really. Plenty of AI-assisted pages came through fine. The ones that died had no first-hand detail, no original photos and no numbers you couldn't find somewhere else. The pages that got punished were the ones that added nothing new, no matter who typed them.

What the Operators Who Survived Did Differently

The founders I advise who got through spring 2024 in one piece had one thing in common. Search was a channel for them, never the whole company. One runs a supplement store that got maybe 30% of revenue from organic. She took a hit and kept going, because her 22,000-person email list still opened every Tuesday. It's the same lesson merchants learned during Shopify's 2020 breakout year: own the customer relationship and treat every other channel as rented. For more on this, see Apple's ATT Privacy Update in 2021 Broke Online Advertising and Forced a Smarter Era.

In the months after the rollout, the survivors did this:

  • Cut or merged pages that had no traffic and nothing unique to say. One client deleted 212 of 340 posts and saw partial recovery by July.
  • Added proof that a human did the work, like original photos, test notes, the price actually paid and dates.
  • Put budget into an email list and a lead magnet before chasing new keywords.
  • Shut down rented subfolders and coupon partnerships before the May 5 enforcement date.

The part nobody wants to hear

Deleting content doesn't guarantee a comeback. Google's own core update guidance says improvements can take months to show up, and sometimes only after the next core update. I've watched sites do everything right and stay flat anyway. And if someone sells you a "March update recovery package" with a guaranteed timeline... walk away. Nobody outside Google can promise that, and most people inside it can't either.

The Bottom Line

Do this today. Open Search Console, set the range to the last 16 months and export your top 50 pages by clicks. Next to each one, write the single thing on that page a reader couldn't get from the top three competing results. If you can't fill that column for more than half your pages, you know what next month's work is. And if organic search brings in more than half your revenue, start building your email list this week, before the next update decides for you.

Frequently asked questions

Did the March 2024 Google update penalize AI-written content?

No, not directly. Google's scaled content abuse policy targets pages made mainly to rank, whether a person or an AI wrote them. AI-assisted pages with original detail and real experience often came through fine.

How long does it take to recover from the March 2024 core update?

Usually months, and sometimes recovery only shows up after the next core update. Google's own guidance says this, and some sites never fully come back even after cleaning up their content.

What is site reputation abuse in Google's spam policy?

It's when a site with strong authority hosts third-party pages, like coupon or product review sections, mainly to borrow that authority for rankings. Google started enforcing it on May 5, 2024.
