How to Validate a Business Idea Without Spending a Dollar

By Ryan Cho

Photo by Anna Shvets / Pexels

Most first-time founders spend money before they've earned the right to. They build the product, buy the domain, hire a designer, maybe even file an LLC, and only then go looking for customers. That's not validation. That's hope with a credit card attached. I've watched this pattern play out dozens of times in the founders I advise, and the result is almost always the same: a polished product that real people don't actually want to pay for.

Here's my position, and I'll argue it plainly: you can get a reliable read on whether a business idea has legs before you spend a single dollar. Not a perfect read. Not a guaranteed one. But enough signal to decide if the next step is worth taking. The tools exist. The audiences are already assembled. What's missing, for most people, is the willingness to do the unglamorous work of talking to strangers and sitting with uncomfortable feedback. So let's get into the actual mechanics.

Start With the Problem, Not the Product

Before you validate a solution, you need to confirm the problem is real and that people are actively suffering from it. Not mildly inconvenienced. Actually losing time, money, or sleep over it. There's a difference between a problem people mention and one they're already paying someone else to solve badly. That gap is where real businesses live.

The free version of this research is Reddit, Quora, and niche Facebook groups. Search the problem you think you're solving. Look at threads where people vent, ask for help, or share workarounds. When I was exploring the idea for my second company, a B2B SaaS product, I spent about two weeks just reading forum threads before writing a single line of code. I counted recurring complaints, noted the language people used, and identified which parts of the problem generated the most frustration. That qualitative data shaped the entire product roadmap.

According to a 2019 CB Insights post-mortem analysis of 101 startup failures, 42 percent cited "no market need" as the primary cause of failure. That's not a funding problem or an execution problem. It's a validation problem. And it's almost entirely avoidable if you do the upfront work of confirming the problem exists at scale before building anything.

Use Real Buyer Conversations Instead of Surveys

Surveys feel like validation. They're not. When you ask someone "would you use this product," you're asking them to predict their own future behavior, which humans are notoriously bad at. You'll get a lot of "yes, definitely" responses from people who would never open their wallets. It's a classic misconception that survey enthusiasm translates to actual demand. It rarely does.

What works is a specific kind of direct conversation, one where you're not pitching anything. You're just trying to understand their world. I use a loose version of Rob Fitzpatrick's Mom Test framework, where you ask about the past rather than the hypothetical future. "How do you currently handle this?" "What did you try last time?" "How much did you spend on that?" Those answers give you real data. And if someone volunteers that they'd pay to solve the problem without you asking, that's a strong signal.

You can find these conversations for free. Post in relevant communities that you're researching a problem and want to talk to people who experience it. Offer nothing in return. Aim for 15 to 20 conversations before you draw any conclusions. It's time-intensive, yes. But it costs zero dollars and it'll save you from building the wrong thing for six months.

Build a Fake Door and Measure What Happens

A fake door test is simple. You put up a landing page, a social post, or even a plain text description of your product, and you point people at it to see if they take a meaningful action. Signing up for a waitlist. Clicking a "buy now" button. Filling out an interest form. The action matters more than what you say to drive it.

You don't need to spend money on this. Carrd.co has a free tier that's perfectly functional for a basic landing page. Google Forms handles signups. You can drive early traffic by posting directly in communities, sharing in your own network, or even cold-emailing people who match your target profile. When I tested the concept for my third company, I wrote a single post in a niche Slack community describing the problem I was solving and asking if anyone dealt with it. Forty-seven people responded in 48 hours. That was enough to keep going.

One honest caveat here: fake door tests work better for some business types than others. If you're targeting a technically sophisticated or skeptical audience, a rough landing page might get dismissed before they engage with the idea at all. In those cases, a direct conversation or a detailed written proposal sent to a small number of carefully chosen prospects will outperform a public landing page. Know your audience before you pick your method.

The Pre-Sale Is the Only Validation That Really Counts

Everything above gets you closer to confidence. But the only real validation is money changing hands. And you can pursue this without having anything built yet, if you're willing to be honest with people about what you're doing. A pre-sale isn't a scam. It's a commitment device for both parties. You're telling the buyer: I believe this is real enough to take your money, and I'm giving you first access in return.

You don't need a payment processor. Some of the earliest pre-sales I've seen founders run were done via bank transfer, PayPal, or literally a handshake agreement to invoice later. The mechanism isn't the point. The point is finding out if someone will commit actual value, not just enthusiasm, to the idea. Even three or four paying customers before you build anything gives you more useful information than a hundred survey responses.

So here's my actual editorial position on all of this: validation isn't a phase you pass through. It's an ongoing practice. Even after you've launched, you should be continuously testing assumptions against real behavior. The founders who treat early validation as a checkbox to tick before getting to the "real work" are the ones who end up building something the market doesn't want. The ones who stay curious, stay skeptical of their own assumptions, and keep asking hard questions, those are the ones who build businesses that last. And most of that mindset costs nothing at all.

Today, before you do anything else: pick the one assumption your business idea depends on most, whether it's that the problem is painful enough, that people will pay a certain price, or that your target customer even exists at scale. Then figure out the cheapest, fastest way to test just that assumption. Not the whole idea. Not the full product. Just that one thing. Do it before the end of the week. That's the discipline that separates founders who eventually build something real from those who stay in planning mode indefinitely.
