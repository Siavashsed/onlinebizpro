How to Find Your First 100 Customers Without Paid Ads (And Why Most Founders Skip the Hard Part)

Serial entrepreneur Siavash Sadighi shares the exact approach to finding your first 100 customers without spending a dollar on ads.

Most founders waste their first six months building features nobody asked for, then panic and dump money into Facebook ads. I've watched this cycle play out dozens of times. The ads don't work because the product hasn't been proven yet, the targeting is a guess, and the messaging is generic. They burn through a few thousand dollars, get a handful of signups who never convert, and conclude that "marketing is expensive." It isn't. They just skipped the part where you find customers by hand.

Start With the 50 People Already in Your Phone

Before you touch Reddit, LinkedIn, or any community, open your contacts. Every founder I've worked with underestimates how many relevant people they already know. You don't need to pitch everyone. You need to identify the 40 to 50 people who work in the industry you're targeting, or who match your buyer profile, and have an honest conversation with them. Not a sales call. A conversation where you ask what they're struggling with and whether your solution sounds useful.

When I was building my second business, a B2B SaaS tool for logistics teams, my first 11 paying customers came entirely from second-degree connections. I asked 30 people I knew if they could introduce me to someone who dealt with the problem I was solving. Around 18 said yes. Of those introductions, 11 converted to paid within 60 days. No ads, no cold outreach software, no growth hacking. Just warm conversations.

The reason this works is trust transfer. A warm introduction from someone they respect compresses the sales cycle significantly. According to a Nielsen study from 2022, 88% of people trust recommendations from people they know over any other form of marketing. That statistic hasn't stopped being true. And yet founders keep ignoring their own networks in favor of tactics that require zero personal vulnerability.

Go Where Your Customers Are Already Complaining

Reddit, niche Facebook groups, Slack communities, Discord servers, and industry forums are full of people describing the exact problem you solve, sometimes in the exact language you should be using in your own copy. Your job isn't to spam them. It's to show up consistently, contribute real answers, and let curiosity do the selling.

The tactic that worked best for me was what I call "answer first, mention second." I'd find threads where someone was frustrated with a specific problem, write a genuinely useful reply that solved part of their issue, and then mention my product only if it addressed the rest. Not as a pitch. As a footnote. Something like: "I actually built something that handles the second part of this, happy to share if you're curious." The conversion rate on those soft mentions was significantly higher than any cold DM campaign I've tested.

There's a common misconception here worth addressing directly: most people think community engagement takes months to pay off. In some niches, that's true. But in tighter communities where the same 200 to 500 people are very active, you can become a recognizable name in three to four weeks of consistent participation. The key word is consistent. Posting once and disappearing is worse than not posting at all.

Cold Outreach Still Works, But Only If You Do It Right

Cold email and cold LinkedIn messages have a terrible reputation because most people use them badly. Generic templates, no personalization, immediate pitch, zero context. That approach deserves to fail. But personalized, research-heavy outreach to a small, well-targeted list is still one of the most efficient ways to get early customers, especially in B2B.

The rule I follow: never send more than 20 cold messages at once. Instead, spend 10 minutes on each person before you write a single word. Find something specific about them, their recent post, a company milestone, a challenge specific to their role, and lead with that. The message should be three sentences or fewer. State what you noticed, connect it to a problem you solve, and ask one simple question. Not "can I get 30 minutes of your time." Something that requires almost no effort to answer.

In my work with early-stage founders, the ones who consistently get 30 to 40 percent reply rates on cold outreach are the ones who send fewer messages and research more deeply. It's slower. But 15 replies from 40 targeted messages beats 8 replies from 400 blasted emails, because those 15 people are actually qualified and the conversations that follow are real.

Content That Attracts Buyers, Not Just Readers

Writing content sounds like a long game, and to some extent it is. But there's a version of content that works quickly if you aim it correctly. The mistake most founders make is writing about their product or their industry broadly. That attracts curious readers. What attracts buyers is content that addresses the specific problem your product solves, written with enough specificity that the right person reads it and thinks "this person understands my situation exactly."

One post, one video, or one LinkedIn article that nails a specific pain point can generate inbound interest within days if you share it in the right communities. I've seen a single 800-word LinkedIn post drive 60 profile visits and 12 DM conversations in 48 hours, purely because it named a problem precisely and offered a clear point of view on how to fix it. The distribution was manual: the founder shared it in four relevant groups and tagged three people who'd mentioned the problem before. That's it.

So the editorial position I'd push you toward is this: treat content as a precision tool, not a volume play. One piece of genuinely useful, tightly targeted content distributed manually to the right people will outperform a content calendar full of generic posts every time. Especially when you're trying to find your first 100 customers, not build an audience of thousands.

The Bottom Line

Here's what you can do today: open your contacts, identify 20 people who match your buyer profile or know someone who does, and send them a message asking for a 15-minute conversation. Not a pitch, just a conversation. That one action, repeated across your network over the next few weeks, will likely get you further than any ad campaign you could run with a $500 budget. The first 100 customers are a manual process. Accept that, and you'll get there faster than you think.
